02 — A SHORT HISTORY

Every summit has
a story behind it.

The history of mountaineering is more than summit dates. It includes local knowledge, mountain communities, evolving equipment and changing ideas about exploration.

1786

Mont Blanc opens a new chapter

Jacques Balmat and Michel-Gabriel Paccard complete the first recorded ascent of Mont Blanc, a landmark in the development of modern alpinism.

1857

Climbing communities organize

The Alpine Club is founded in the United Kingdom, helping formalize a shared community for mountaineering knowledge and exploration.

1953

Everest's first confirmed ascent

Tenzing Norgay and Edmund Hillary reach the summit on 29 May, supported by a wider expedition team and generations of Himalayan expertise.

TODAY

Skill, science and stewardship

Modern alpinists combine updated equipment standards, better forecasts and training with a growing responsibility to protect fragile mountain environments.

Milestones give context; they are not a measure of personal readiness.

03 — CHOOSE YOUR APPROACH

Different routes.
Different disciplines.

There is no single way to climb a mountain. Terrain, weather, objective, access and team experience all influence the style of ascent.

Training note Difficulty varies by route and conditions. Use local grading systems, take courses and progress gradually instead of relying on a label alone.

04 — SKILLS THAT TRAVEL

Strong systems.
Clear decisions.

Technical skills only help when they are practised, checked and matched to the situation. Learn with a qualified mountain instructor before relying on them in consequential terrain.

Review the field checklist

Route-finding & navigation

Read maps, terrain and weather together. Carry a reliable navigation backup and know how to use it in poor visibility.

01

Ropework & protection

Learn knots, belaying, anchor concepts and partner checks in supervised practice. Choose equipment for the intended system.

02

Crampon & ice-axe movement

Practise on appropriate low-angle terrain with instruction, including safe handling, footwork and the limits of self-arrest.

03

Emergency response & rescue

Train for likely hazards on your route, communicate an emergency plan and know local rescue procedures before you go.

05 — REST & RECOVERY

Shelter is part of
the route plan.

A safe night depends on weather, exposure, group condition and access. Select a shelter strategy before leaving, and always have a realistic contingency.

06 — KNOW WHAT CAN CHANGE

Respect the risk.
Keep options open.

Mountains are dynamic environments. A conservative decision made early is often safer than a desperate decision made late.

WEATHER

Rapid weather shifts

Wind, storms, whiteout, cold and wet clothing can turn a manageable route serious. Monitor forecasts and retreat early.

ALTITUDE & COLD

Altitude illness & hypothermia

Know warning signs, ascend gradually and descend when symptoms or exposure make continuing unsafe.

GLACIERS

Crevasses & hidden bridges

Glacier travel requires appropriate roped-team skills, rescue training, route knowledge and current local information.

SNOWPACK

Avalanches

Get formal avalanche education, read the local bulletin, carry and practise with rescue equipment, and choose conservative terrain.

ROCK & ICE

Rockfall and icefall

Assess overhead hazard, exposure, temperature and timing. Helmets reduce some injury risks but cannot make terrain safe.

HUMAN FACTORS

Fatigue & summit pressure

Track pace, hydration, daylight and group morale. A summit is optional; a safe return is the objective.

This is general educational information, not a complete safety manual or emergency advice.
